Security through fairly shared co-responsibility (and accountability).

To reduce the risks posed to children & adolescents by social media, AI, or apps, further regulatory action is required.

This also entails clear rules, particularly for large tech companies ("Very Large Online Platforms"). This does not mean restricting Europe’s—often smaller—technology firms; rather, it ensures that fair rules apply to everyone, helping to foster a mentally healthier online environment for users. Even now, the EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) and the Digital Markets Act are already lagging behind current regulatory requirements. For this reason alone, clear labeling obligations should be implemented without delay, enabling users to identify content that is mentally harmful or addictive.

Regulation still has long ways to go before children become safe. We think some of these ideas might interest you.

Platforms that serve children and families must be designed for wellbeing, not compulsion. Regulation should shift the default — from engagement-maximising dark patterns toward architectures that give users genuine control over their time, attention, and information diet.

Addictive design
Ban compulsive-engagement features for minors by default
Require platforms to disable infinite scroll, autoplay, and night-time push notifications for users under 18 as a default setting, not an opt-in. Effective controls must introduce real friction — not dismissible pop-ups.

Personalisation
Mandate user-controllable algorithm settings
Users should be able to reduce hyper-personalisation, limit content repetition, and filter out clickbait-heavy or sensationalist sources. Recommender systems must offer a chronological or reduced-curation mode as a genuine alternative.

Parental tools
Require robust, low-friction parental controls on major platforms
Parent-accessible usage-time limits, content filters, and opt-outs must be straightforward to activate without requiring technical expertise. Platforms must not design these controls to be burdensome or easily circumvented.

Labeling
Introduce mandatory labeling for dark patterns and synthetic content
Platforms must clearly disclose dark-pattern mechanisms (streak pressure, reward loops, FOMO triggers) at point of use. AI-generated content, bot accounts, and outlets with a pattern of misinformation must be systematically labeled, not left to voluntary disclosure.

Bot activity
Prohibit bots and agents from posing as human users
Automated accounts participating in social interactions, comment sections, or gaming platforms must be identifiable as non-human at all times. Platforms bear responsibility for proactively detecting and flagging undisclosed bot activity.

Transparency
Strengthen content moderation transparency and reporting mechanisms
Platforms must publish regular, auditable reports on content moderation outcomes. Reporting tools for harmful content must be easy to find and result in timely, traceable action. Arbitrary account suspensions require clear written reasoning and a meaningful appeals process.

AI systems increasingly shape what children see, learn, and believe — often invisibly. Regulation must ensure that automated decisions affecting minors are explainable, auditable, and subordinate to meaningful human oversight, particularly where wellbeing and cognitive development are at stake.

Explainability
Require explainability for all automated curation decisions
Any AI-driven decision that shapes a user's content feed, search results, or recommendations must be explainable on request. Users — and parents of minors — have the right to understand why content is surfaced, and to contest or override that logic.

Filter bubbles
Regulate AI systems that create epistemic filter bubbles
Recommender systems must be assessed for their tendency to narrow information exposure or reinforce skewed worldviews, particularly for younger users. Platforms should be required to periodically introduce content diversity signals into feeds, especially for educational or civic topics.

Human oversight
Mandate human review for high-stakes AI decisions affecting minors
Automated decisions with significant consequences — account actions, age-gating, content restriction — must include a meaningful human review option. Fully automated decision chains without recourse should be prohibited in contexts where children are involved.

Risk assessment
Require child-specific AI risk assessments before deployment
AI features deployed in consumer-facing platforms must undergo documented risk assessments for their effects on minors' mental health, cognitive habits, and information diet — prior to launch, and periodically thereafter. Results should be available to regulators on request.

Synthetic content
Establish provenance standards for AI-generated content in public feeds
AI-generated images, video, audio, and text distributed via major platforms must carry machine-readable provenance metadata. Platforms must surface this information to users in a clear, non-technical way — particularly where content may be mistaken for authentic human expression.

Navigating today's digital environment is a core life skill. Schools, families, and public institutions share responsibility for equipping children — and parents — with the critical thinking, emotional resilience, and practical tools needed to participate online safely and confidently.

Curriculum
Embed digital and media literacy into the national curriculum from primary school
Age-appropriate digital literacy — including how algorithms work, how to identify misinformation, and how platforms are designed to capture attention — should be a required part of schooling from early primary years, not a late add-on.

Critical thinking
Prioritise lateral thinking and source verification skills
Teach children to verify claims across multiple independent sources before accepting them as true — a practice used by professional fact-checkers. This includes practical exercises in spotting clickbait, emotional manipulation, and AI-generated misinformation.

Digital resilience
Include digital wellbeing and resilience education as standard
Children should learn to recognise compulsive usage patterns, understand the psychological mechanics of reward loops and FOMO, and develop personal strategies for healthy screen-time habits. This connects directly to broader mental health education.

Parent support
Fund public programmes to support parents in digital parenting
Parents need accessible, practical guidance on platform controls, screen time, and online risks — not just policy documents. Schools and community organisations should be supported to run regular parent workshops, and platforms should be required to contribute to public digital literacy funding.

Teacher training
Invest in ongoing digital literacy training for teachers
Educators cannot teach what they don't know. Regular, funded professional development on digital platforms, AI tools, and online risk should be a standard part of teacher training — updated as the technology landscape changes.

Existing rules too often go unenforced for years, while platforms continue profiting from harmful designs. Effective accountability means faster action, penalties that genuinely change behaviour, and transparent processes that allow families and civil society to hold platforms to account.

Speed
Establish fast-track enforcement procedures for child safety violations
Investigations into violations directly affecting minors should operate on shortened timelines with interim protective measures — including mandatory labeling requirements or feature suspensions — applied while full proceedings are ongoing, rather than waiting years for final rulings.

Penalties
Ensure fines are large enough to change platform behaviour
Penalties that represent a rounding error in platform revenues do not change incentives. Fines should be calculated as a meaningful percentage of global turnover — as envisaged under DSA Article 74 — and escalate for repeated violations. Political delays in imposing final fines must not become a loophole.

Interim measures
Use mandatory labeling as an immediate enforcement lever
Where full legal proceedings are slow, regulators should compel platforms to apply prominent user-facing disclosures to features under investigation — dark patterns, unverified accounts, algorithmic amplification — as a low-barrier interim step that protects users without awaiting a final ruling.

Auditing
Require independent algorithmic audits for large platforms
Major platforms should be subject to mandatory, independent third-party audits of their recommender systems and risk mitigation measures — particularly for child-facing features. Audit results should be published in a standardised, comparable format accessible to the public.

Access & appeals
Create clear, accessible channels for complaint and appeal
Families and users must have a simple, documented route to report harmful content or contest platform decisions — including account suspensions. Platforms must respond within defined timeframes and provide written reasoning. Regulator-run escalation paths should exist where platform responses are inadequate.

Ein digitaler Kompass, der Kinder praxisnah vorbereitet. Bei Online-Risiken denken viele Eltern zuerst an:

  • Fremde Personen und gefährliche Chats
  • Cybermobbing / Hatespeech
  • Bildschirmzeit und App-Sucht
  • Passwörter und Phishing

Natürlich sind diese alle wichtig.

Doch Risiken sind ebenso: Abhängig machende Benutzungsmuster; Desinformation beschleunigende Algorithmen, Verzerrte Realität durch KI-Inhalte, FOMO und Gruppendruck, manipulative Mechanismen, KI-gesteuerte Bots in Chats und somit veränderte Gewohnheiten, die Verhalten und Selbstverständnis langfristig beeinflussen. Damit Kinder ihr Online-sein nicht unkontrolliert+unkritisch unter digitalem Dauerfeuer verbringen, werden notwendige Kompetenzen mit praxisnahen Trainingsaufgaben vermittelt.

Die Spielregeln ändern sich täglich.
Neue Plattformen. Neue Influencer Trends. Neue Risiken.

Es ist schwer am Ball zu bleiben, schließlich haben Eltern nicht die Zeit jeder neuen Entwicklung hinterherzuhetzen. Deshalb gibt Cybear auch Eltern kompakte Informationsformate in die Hand.

Eltern erhalten kurz-gehaltene Erklärvideos, ebenso alters-adäquate Checklists (wie reglementiere ich gemeinsam, was sollte ich ansprechen) und Verhaltens-Guidelines. Kurz und praktisch an der Realität der Eltern orientiert, keine langen Textwände und Abhandlungen.
