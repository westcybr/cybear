Media Literacy Guide for Parents

Digital Skills
by Age Group

Where do you even start with so many different topics thrown at you? From "What is a password?" to dealing with cyberbullying and spotting AI bias: kids need completely different skills depending on their developmental stage. This roadmap highlights what is truly relevant at every age and gives you the best ways to bring it up naturally.
*If you run into any unfamiliar tech terms, just look them up in our Media Literacy Glossary.

Ages 6 – 8

Primary School · First Explorations

Laying down the first digital building blocks. Keep rules concrete, playful, and completely fear-free.

Topics
What to do / How to talk about it
Tips & Dos/Don'ts
What is the Internet?
Explain it simply: The internet is like a massive, infinite city full of tools for creating, chatting, and learning. Just like any city, people can use it for good things or bad things.
Show them that there are real people behind those screens—and remind them that some individuals might not be nice or could have motives completely different from what they claim.
Explain the boundary between Online and Offline: Whatever happens online can still cause very real feelings, and online situations can easily spill over into their actual, day-to-day lives.
💡 Use visuals or quick videos to explain. Avoid shouting: "Don't go on that site!", try this instead: "It's just like a big city—most people are nice, but you still stay close to a trusted adult."
Passwords & Privacy
Frame passwords as personal "superpowers." They protect our stuff and should never be shared with anyone—not even their best friends.
Teach them that their name, age, school, and home address are private information (like hidden treasures!) that should never be given away without a parent's okay.
Talk about avatars, profile pictures, and usernames: Explain that they don't have to show their real face online. Flip side: if someone else shows a photo, it might not actually be who they say they are.
⚠️ Don't overdo the scare tactics! We don't want kids terrified of the web; we just want them to understand what privacy means. Invent a fun password together and write it down securely... ideally directly into your Family Media Agreement.
Good vs. Bad Experiences
Differentiate between a "good surprise" and an "unsafe secret": If anyone online asks them to hide something from Mom or Dad, tell them to report it to you right away.
Practice intuition: "How does your tummy feel when something seems a bit weird?" Teach them to trust that gut feeling!
Reassure them constantly: "You will absolutely never get into trouble with me for telling me about something that happened online."
💡 Role-playing works wonders: "What do you do if a stranger in a game asks what your name is?" Keep it light rather than scary, but talk through these scenarios concretely.
Body & Digital Safety
Use correct anatomical terms (learn them together!): Make it clear that nobody is ever allowed to ask for photos of body parts that are normally covered by a swimsuit.
Both online and offline, the rule is absolute: no safe adult will ever ask a child for those kinds of photos. These private areas must be guarded like secret treasures.
State it clearly: If someone asks, it is never your fault. Just close the app and come straight to Mom or Dad.
⚠️ Keep your tone neutral and calm. No dramatic gasps. Kids at this age handle this information beautifully when it's explained in a matter-of-fact, age-appropriate way.
Screen Time & Habits
Break down active vs. passive screen time: Drawing, coding, or learning online isn't the same as scrolling mindlessly or binging videos for hours. Remind them their brain still grows best interacting with the physical world.
Establish bedrooms and bedtime as strict screen-free zones, ideally cutting off devices at least 1 hour before sleep.
Plan wrap-up times together instead of ripping the device away mid-game. Set clear, pre-agreed expectations for what happens if a tablet session accidentally runs a tiny bit over.
💡 Don't just ask "How long were you on?", switch it to "What cool things did you build or see?" Write these rules down together and hang them up where everyone can see them.
Strangers in Games
Explain that many multiplayer games include other players from around the world, and just because you play together doesn't mean they are automatic friends.
Give them total permission to say "no" or leave if someone acts strange or asks questions that make them feel uncomfortable.
Identify "go-to adults": Who are the trusted people in their life they can tell absolutely anything to?
💡 Statistically, about 1 in 7 primary school kids easily trust strangers inside video games. Tackle this topic directly before they dive into their first multiplayer experiences. It's incredibly hard for kids to tell "friends" apart from strangers online, especially when bad actors use jokes, free virtual gifts, or cool avatars to win them over. The golden rule here: You can't judge someone's true character just because they are acting nice in a chat room.
Ages 9 – 11

Primary School · Growing Independence

Navigating social dynamics, group pressures, and making critical thinking second nature.

Tips & Dos/Don'ts
Digital Footprint
Explain that anything put out on the web can be copied, shared, and stored forever—even if they hit "delete."
Use the billboard test: "Would you be comfortable plastering this on a massive poster right in front of your school gates?"
Google their own name together. Look at what pops up and see what kind of context or random search results appear.
⚠️ Try not to scold them for things they might have already posted. We want to build awareness here, not shame. Use the phrase "A screenshot means forever" to make it stick. Remind them that posting something mean, embarrassing, or false about a classmate can stay out there indefinitely, turning them into a creator of harm with digital tracks that never truly vanish.
Facts vs. Opinions
Practice separating opinions, beliefs, and cold hard facts using real-life examples from your day-to-day routine.
Break down misinformation: Just because a whole bunch of online voices are repeating something loudly doesn't make it true.
Point out ads and sneaky clickbait: Why is this title so dramatic? What exactly is it trying to get you to click or buy?
💡 Use a trending news headline or post as a mini family game. Ask: "What part of this is actually verified?" Flip the script sometimes—let the kids twist a true story on purpose and see if you can spot the fake details. It's fun and sharpens their radar.
Group Chats & Social Dynamics
Walk through chat room realities: Seeking approval, handling misunderstandings, peer exclusion, and rapid-fire chain reactions feel just as intense over text as they do on the playground.
Talk about "phone fatigue": Constant pings and unread notification numbers can stress our brains out and drain our mental battery. It is completely okay to turn them off.
Foster digital empathy and moral courage: What can you do if you notice a classmate is being left out, picked on, or targeted in the class group chat?
⚠️ Never dismiss their online drama with lines like, "Oh, it's just silly kids' stuff in a group text." That completely invalidates their genuine experiences. Instead, take it seriously and help them brainstorm how to react or handle the situation constructively.
Cyberbullying & Reporting
Discuss where the line is: What constitutes genuine cyberbullying versus someone just leaving a one-off "silly or mean comment"?
Show them exactly how to use the block button, file an in-app report, and grab a screenshot (as essential proof).
Reinforce this mindset: Reporting a bad actor isn't being a "snitch" or a tattle-tale; it is a protective action that keeps the whole community safe.
💡 Sit down with the specific apps or platforms your child uses. Find the report tool together right now so they know exactly where it lives before any stressful situation happens.
Scams & Fake Profiles
Explain why certain people create fake accounts online to pretend to be someone they aren't.
Teach the basic rule of thumb: If it sounds "too good to be true," it's a scam. Watch out for free Robux offers, suspicious giveaways, rare items, or random links sent in chats.
Show them what phishing looks like. How do scammers mimic trusted brands or people to lower your guard? What tiny details give away a fake account or an identity thief?
💡 Pull examples straight from the games they play (Roblox, Minecraft, WhatsApp). Use "What would you do if..." as a quick, casual scenario game while making dinner.
Copyright & Sharing
Explain that pictures, tracks, and videos floating around the web usually belong to the creator who made them.
Clarify what is totally fine to download, share, or repurpose versus what might violate rules or laws.
Sharing photos of their peers: Make it a habit that they must ask for their friend's explicit permission before sending or posting their picture.
💡 Tie it to real school tasks: "Hey, that cool image you added to your presentation slide... do you know who originally drew it or owns it?"
In-App Purchases & Hook Mechanics
Explain how loot boxes, cosmetic character skins, and premium in-game currencies operate, and why developers design them to be so incredibly tempting.
Practice impulse control with a simple rule: "Let's wait exactly 24 hours. If you still want it tomorrow, we can talk about it then."
Open up your device settings together and configure purchase protection passwords so no accidental buying can happen.
⚠️ Don't just hand down a flat "No." Explain the psychological tricks behind why these microtransactions are designed to be almost impossible to resist. Building that inner filter protects them far better than any software block.
Ages 12 – 14

Early Teens · Decoding Invisible Forces

Algorithms, AI tools, and handling complex online scenarios: This is where deeper comprehension becomes vital.

Topics
What to do / How to talk about it
Tips & Dos/Don'ts
Algorithms & Filter Bubbles
Explain how the feed operates: The app tracks everything you linger on and starts serving up more of the same content, even if it makes you feel anxious or upset. Point out that controversial or negative content gets the highest engagement.
Demystify filter bubbles and echo chambers: Why are you only seeing one side of an issue? Encourage them to think about how many perspectives exist around a single "truth."
Show them how to actively disrupt the code: follow accounts with completely different viewpoints and intentionally look up mixed topics to reset their algorithm.
💡 Look through your own TikTok or YouTube feeds side-by-side: "Why do you think the system chose this exact clip for us right now?" Pull back the curtain on data tracking—remind them the app logs exactly how many seconds they linger on a video, what creator they click, and what hour of the day it is.
AI Literacy
Audit AI-generated text or answers critically. Build the habit of cross-referencing claims against established, independent sources.
Explain AI "hallucinations": Chatbots frequently fabricate convincing facts, historical events, or fake citations. Even if the results look funny, trusting them blindly can get tricky.
Keep them grounded: 84% of teens believe they can effortlessly tell an AI bot apart from a human user, yet research proves most are regularly fooled. Confidence does not mean competence.
💡 Test an AI tool together on a niche topic or family history fact you know inside and out, then spot the errors. Make the illusion visible. Warn them that basic profiles in comment sections, forums, or chats are often automated AI bots pushing specific agendas, collecting user info, or mimicking human speech patterns flawlessly.
AI Friendships & Parasocial Bonds
Realize that many teens use AI companion apps to process deep personal problems, sometimes talking to bots more than real-world friends.
Unpack the tech: An AI chatbot is simply a mirror that loops back exactly what you want to hear. It feels comforting at first, but it isn't an authentic connection. It's an empty machine algorithmically adapting to your specific word choices without feeling any actual emotion.
Chatbots are trained to sound intensely curious, validation-heavy, and interested in you. In reality, these are just predictive phrase formulas generated to sound empathetic and flatter the user.
AI can offer wonderful, judgment-free, instant advice without the messy ego or arrogance of humans. However, leaning on it too much can make navigating the organic friction of real-world human conversations feel overwhelming.
💡 Fire up a major LLM chat interface like Mistral, OpenAI, Claude, Gemini, or Perplexity together. Run a few experiments: feed it false premises on purpose or try out creative roleplay scenarios (like convincing an AI "mad professor" to give up a secret vault code). Show them how the software processes language patterns dynamically.
Consent, Explicit Media & Sexting Risks
Digital consent applies across the board: forward-sharing memes, passing along group photos, or leaking screenshots. The golden rule is always to ask first; never assume it's fine to share.
Dismantle the Snapchat myth: Even if media "disappears" after a few seconds, it can easily be saved via external devices or recorded without their knowledge.
Make sure they understand that sharing, possessing, or forwarding explicit material under the age of 18 (including images of themselves) can carry serious legal consequences.
⚠️ Keep this talk objective and legal-centric. The core goal: Your child needs to understand the real-world liabilities clearly, ensuring they feel safe coming to you for help rather than hiding it out of shame if something goes wrong. Walk them through reporting mechanisms and user rights calmly.
Grooming & Sextortion
Explain how grooming works: It rarely starts out creepy. It typically begins innocently with online gifts, compliments, shared gaming interests, and gradual trust-building.
Tackle sextortion directly: Scammers frequently use basic AI tools to generate fake explicit images to blackmail targets. This is a highly organized crime; the young person is the victim, never the one to blame.
Reiterate this safety net: "No matter what happened or what choices you made leading up to it, you will never get in trouble with me for reporting extortion or safety issues to me."
⚠️ This is a heavy, stressful topic—deliver it calmly, clearly, and without panic. The single most crucial sentence your child needs to hear is: "You are not to blame, and my door is always completely open to help you fix this." Educate them on the legal realities of extortion and how to handle it securely.
Body Image & Social Comparison
Remind them frequently: The overwhelming majority of photos across social platforms are heavily filtered, strategically staged, or fully edited. An uploaded photo is usually one selection out of a hundred attempts—you are viewing an artificial highlight reel.
Understand FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) and social comparison: Help them check in with themselves: "How do I feel after scrolling through this feed for 20 minutes? Do I feel energized, or do I feel down?"
Foster healthy internal dialogue: Teach them to anchor their self-worth in real-world strengths rather than comparing their daily life to polished, curated digital imagery.
💡 Review a few popular accounts together: "What is this specific profile trying to sell or convey? How does looking at it make us feel?" Developing an objective, critical lens toward digital content is a skill that can be trained over time.
Ages 15 – 18

Late Teens · Personal Responsibility

Shifting the focus to long-term consequences, data ownership, and emotional self-regulation.

Topics
What to do / How to talk about it
Tips & Dos/Don'ts
Digital Reputation & The Future
Remind them that future employers and colleges routinely vet social profiles; thoughtless old content can easily slam important doors shut.
Team up to "clean up" and intentionally curate their public digital footprint together.
Look into professional networks like LinkedIn: What belongs on a professional profile, and why does it matter?
💡 Skip the moral lecture. Concrete real-world scenarios or case studies impact teens much more than vague warnings. Try: "Did you see this article about the student who lost their college acceptance or job over an old post...?"
Mental Health & Social Media
Help them spot early warning signs: dynamic FOMO, body image anxieties, or feeling socially disconnected and drained after logging off.
Pitch a "Social Media Detox" experiment: How does it feel to step away from specific high-trigger apps for just one week?
Encourage them to evaluate the apps that make them feel insecure or unhappy—and give them permission to hit delete.
⚠️ Don't force it down from above; frame it as an open invite: "Want to try an app detox week together?" Doing it as a family lowers their defenses. Make sure to call out how heavily bodies and sex appeal are warped online, using editing tricks and filters to fake an unachievable perfection.
Privacy & Data Sovereignty
Dig into what tracking data major platforms actually scrape from our daily routines, and where that data gets sold.
Switch on Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) and audit third-party app logins to revoke permissions they don't use anymore.
Go through safety settings on all their active accounts side-by-side to plug the leaks.
💡 Turn a "Privacy Night" into an easy family ritual: once a year, sit down together to audit accounts, update passwords, and adjust permissions.
Advanced AI Verification
When researching critical areas (like medical, legal, or major breaking news), prompt multiple AI models and cross-check the discrepancies.
Watch out for cut-off dates: Remind them that an LLM's dataset has a specific knowledge expiration date.
Use official government sites, peer-reviewed journals, and scientific sources to double-verify claims.
💡 An AI is an assistant with structural weaknesses, not an all-knowing oracle. Mastering how to fact-check its output is one of the single most valuable career skills this generation can learn.
Misinformation & Political Manipulation
Unpack deepfakes: Show them how easily AI-manipulated footage and cloned voice notes can seem completely authentic.
Look at targeted political ads: Who is funding this content, what action do they want from you, and why are you the target group?
Introduce them to reverse-image search and reliable independent fact-checking hubs.
💡 Deconstruct a viral clip or trending graphic together. Treat it like a fun detective game to answer: "Is this actually legit or carefully framed?"
Relationships, Consent & Boundaries
Address digital consent in dating: sharing photos, live locations, or account passwords. Where is the line between intimacy and toxic control?
Identify digital warning signs: recognizing if a partner is using tracking, constant checks, or text spamming to monitor them.
Encourage them to establish and hold their digital boundaries, even when peer expectations feel intense.
⚠️ Keep these chats conversational and completely judgment-free. The goal isn't to micro-manage their dating life, but to empower your teen to recognize respect and control on their own terms.
